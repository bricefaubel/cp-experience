Connected Portal: three ways forward

01 — The core problem

Stated as a business problem, not a UX problem

Diagnosis

The business is generating valuable demand signals and giving away the value. Connected equipment produces exactly what a dealer's business needs — early warning of failures, evidence for service agreements, and justification for replacement quotes — but the portal is built as a system of record, not a system of action. It waits to be visited, defaults itself to silence, and hands the dealer raw fault codes instead of decisions.

The result is that Carrier absorbs the cost of the connected infrastructure while the revenue it enables is realized inconsistently or not at all. Low daily engagement is not the problem; it is the symptom of a product that has no daily job.

02 — Current state

Five dimensions, assessed

Clarity of purpose
Weak
The landing screen leads with five counters and a search box. Nothing tells a first-time dealer what to do — the screen reports inventory, not work.
Task efficiency
Weak
Alert to understanding takes four screens and heavy scrolling, ending at ~50 raw configuration fields in flat alphabetical order — the equipment data model shown as a user interface.
Trust & transparency
Weak
Alerts appear as manufacturer codes with no plain meaning, cause, or action. Duplicates aren't collapsed — one property fired the same fault three times in a minute, so 96 alerts is an unknown number of real problems.
Persona fit
Mixed
Adequate for a technically fluent technician doing post-hoc diagnosis. Weak for the service manager who must decide, in five minutes, which three customers get a truck. Distributors have no roll-up at all.
Business alignment
Weak
Notifications default to None across every alert type. The highest-value capability — predictive malfunction detection — is Pro-gated and manual. 104 opportunities sit disconnected from the alerts that would justify them.
03 — Three strategies

Genuinely divergent, not three shades of the same idea

Each is built on a different belief about why dealers don't engage. Choosing between them is a real strategic decision — they lead to different products, different org dependencies, and different definitions of success.

Option A

The Service Desk

Push-first triage queue
Dealers will never habitually visit a portal, so the portal must arrive in their morning already sorted.
What changes

Notifications default on. Duplicate faults collapse into single incidents. The home screen becomes a ranked list of what needs a human today, in plain language, with a one-click path to dispatch. Everything else moves behind the incident.

Best case

The first screen a service manager opens each morning. Fault-to-dispatch time drops; inbound "my system is making a noise" calls decline because the dealer called first.

Primary risk

Turning notifications on before deduplication and ranking are right floods dealers with noise and they mute permanently. A one-shot trust event.

Option B

The Book of Business

Account health and revenue tool
Dealers engage with money, not machines; the unit of value is the customer relationship, not the control.
What changes

The organising object flips from equipment to household. Each customer carries health, warranty, equipment age, agreement status and revenue at stake. Alerts stop being faults and become commercial triggers.

Best case

The portal becomes the dealer's most valuable business asset rather than a warranty obligation. Connection rates rise because dealers actively want customers on the platform.

Primary risk

Depends on data the portal may not hold — install dates, warranty terms, pricing. Wrong dollar figures destroy credibility faster than no dollar figures.

InvestmentHigh

Option C

The Invisible Portal

Intelligence delivered into existing tools
The right number of portal logins is zero; the signal should appear inside the software dealers already use all day.
What changes

The destination experience is demoted. Diagnostics and predicted failures are pushed as structured records into the dealer's FSM and the technician app — a work order arriving pre-populated. The portal survives as thin administration.

Best case

Adoption stops depending on behaviour change. Value is delivered whether or not anyone thinks about Carrier's portal, and the technician app gets meaningfully stronger.

Primary risk

Carrier becomes a data supplier inside someone else's software, with reduced ability to merchandise Pro tiers or influence the dealer — and a roadmap hostage to third-party integration quality.

InvestmentLow–medium build

06 — Recommendation

Advance Option A first

The position

Fix the silence and the noise before anything else

Option A attacks the root cause directly and cheaply: the portal is silent by default and unsorted when opened. Deduplication, severity ranking, and plain-language alert descriptions are prerequisite work that Options B and C both consume — neither a revenue tool nor an FSM feed is worth building on top of an unranked, duplicated alert stream.

The tradeoff, honestly: Option A improves operations without directly monetising them, so it will show up as engagement and response time, not as revenue, in the first two quarters. An executive expecting a revenue line in Q1 should be choosing B — and should first answer whether the data exists to support it.

Two things worth doing immediately regardless of the decision: change the notification default from None to on (only after deduplication is in place), and remove the manual "Run a Report" step from Malfunction Detection so predictions arrive on their own.

The question the business must answer before committing
Is the FSM integration bidirectional, or read-only?

Every option here depends on a signal turning into scheduled work. If the portal can write a work order back into the dealer's FSM, Option A is a genuine workflow product. If it can only read, all three options end at "we told you" — and the redesign investment should be scoped accordingly, or the integration budget should move ahead of the design budget.

07 — Using these documents

What each one is for

Format

Written as design prompts

Each PRD is structured so it can be handed to a designer — or a generative design tool — and produce screens. Context first, then screen-by-screen requirements, content, integration, and visual direction.

Prototypes

Concept demonstrations, not code

Every interactive element is a working demonstration of a specific argument, built to be clicked in a room. None of it is production code and all figures are illustrative.

Caveat

Priority models are unvalidated

Urgency rankings, health factors, and truncation order are informed proposals drawn from the current product's data — not tested with service managers. Each is cheap to validate and worth doing before design commits.

Data notice

All customer data is fictional

Household names, addresses, phone numbers and equipment serials throughout these documents are invented. Equipment model numbers and fault codes are real, and the patterns shown are drawn from the current product — but no actual customer appears anywhere in this collection.

If presenting live

Open all three on separate tabs

A opens on a queue, B opens on a book, C opens on a record. That is the strategic choice in three images, and it is a reasonable way to start the session before anyone reads a word.
